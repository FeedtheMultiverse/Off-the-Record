MODULES ARE INTERFACES

A TTRPG adventure must function both as something the GM can understand during preparation and as something the GM can operate during play.

Do not assume that evocative prose, complete lore, or a coherent narrative automatically makes a scenario runnable. The GM should never be required to reverse-engineer the adventure's moving parts from paragraphs of prose.

Every major section, investigation node, faction, location, or scenario phase should expose its operational structure clearly.

Never ask only “What is here?” Ask “What is happening here, what can the PCs change, and what changes without them?”

TWO-SPEED DESIGN

Write every adventure for two different reading speeds.

Prep speed:
The GM may want atmosphere, history, character psychology, lore, context, thematic material, and explanations of why events are happening.

Table speed:
The GM needs immediate answers to questions such as:

- What is happening right now?
- Who is active here?
- What does each person or faction want?
- What does each NPC know?
- What are they currently doing?
- What will they do if the PCs intervene?
- What will they do if the PCs do nothing?
- What changes because of the PCs' actions?
- What clues are available?
- Which clues are necessary to keep the investigation moving?
- What events occur next?
- What escalates the situation?
- Where can the players go from here?

The adventure must provide both layers.

Do not make the GM mentally compile the first layer into the second.

EXPOSE THE MACHINE

For every substantial scenario section, provide a concise technical view of how it functions.

Include, where relevant:

Current Situation

What is happening when the PCs arrive?

Describe the unstable situation rather than merely the history that created it.

Active Forces

Identify NPCs, factions, threats, phenomena, institutions, or environmental pressures currently capable of changing events.

For each major actor, specify:

Wants: What are they trying to achieve?

Knows: What useful or misleading information do they possess?

Current action: What are they doing when play begins?

If opposed: How do they react when the PCs interfere?

If aided: What changes if the PCs cooperate with them?

If ignored: What do they do without PC involvement?

Breaking point: What would cause them to change tactics, flee, confess, attack, bargain, panic, escalate, etc.?

NPC descriptions should therefore describe behavior, not only personality.

"Anxious, secretive archivist haunted by what she saw in 1974" is characterization.

"She wants the restricted ledger destroyed, lies about knowing Danny, watches anyone entering the basement stacks, and calls Varga if the PCs photograph the ledger" is runnable information.

Whenever possible, provide both.

CLUES MUST HAVE HANDLES

Do not treat clues as decorative lore.

For every significant clue, establish:

What it tells the players

Where it can be found

Who can reveal it

What action reveals it

What it points toward

Whether it is critical, supporting, optional, misleading, or confirmatory

What happens if the players never obtain it

A clue that does not alter understanding, suggest an action, complicate a decision, reveal danger, or open a route forward may be atmosphere rather than an investigative clue.

Atmosphere is valuable, but do not confuse it with scenario structure.

LORE MUST CHANGE PLAY

Lore becomes memorable when it has consequences.

Whenever introducing important setting information, ask:

What decision does knowing this allow the players to make?

Good lore may:

- reveal a weakness
- change how an NPC can be approached
- explain a strange event
- distinguish one threat from another
- unlock a location
- expose a lie
- suggest a ritual or countermeasure
- reveal factional tensions
- alter the consequences of a choice
- help players predict what will happen next

If lore is important enough to receive substantial page space, strongly prefer giving it a mechanical, investigative, strategic, social, or narrative handle.

DEFAULT TRAJECTORY

Every scenario needs a state describing what happens without the PCs.

The world should not wait motionless for investigators.

Specify:

If the PCs do nothing:
What is the opposition's plan? What event occurs next? Who acts? What deteriorates?

This creates forward pressure and allows the GM to respond naturally when players hesitate, pursue unexpected leads, or abandon an apparent plot hook.

The default trajectory should normally include at least one escalation.

ESCALATION

Investigative scenarios require pressure.

Create an escalation structure showing how the situation changes over time or in response to player actions.

Escalation may be triggered by:

- time passing
- PCs obtaining particular information
- PCs entering certain locations
- enemies becoming aware of the investigation
- NPCs becoming frightened or desperate
- failed attempts to conceal evidence
- supernatural phenomena intensifying
- institutional intervention
- disappearance, injury, contamination, exposure, or death

Do not script an exact sequence of scenes unless the scenario specifically requires one.

Prefer conditional escalation:

"If X happens, Y begins."

"If the PCs reveal Z, the faction changes tactics."

"If nobody intervenes before midnight, A occurs."

This preserves player agency while keeping the scenario alive.

FLOW AND ROUTES

Where useful, provide a simple flowchart, clue map, node map, or dependency diagram.

The diagram should show possible movement through the scenario, not prescribe the correct sequence.

Example:

Initial Hook
→ Station Records
→ Mager Park
→ Missing Photographer's Apartment

Station Records
→ identifies V
→ reveals sealed file
→ points toward Mager Park

Mager Park
→ confirms phenomenon
→ attracts opposition
→ reveals route to final node

Players may reach nodes by different paths.

Do not design mysteries as a chain in which failure to discover one clue causes the scenario to collapse.

CHAPTER / NODE RUN SHEET

When practical, begin substantial scenario sections with a compact GM-facing summary.

Recommended structure:

What is happening:
One or two sentences describing the active situation.

Active NPCs / factions:
Who can affect events here.

Their goals:
What each wants.

Immediate danger or pressure:
What makes the situation unstable.

Critical clues:
Information necessary to maintain scenario connectivity.

Optional clues:
Useful context, shortcuts, warnings, or additional discoveries.

Possible developments:
Likely responses to player actions.

If ignored:
What happens without intervention.

Connections:
Where this section can lead.

Then provide the fuller descriptive material, lore, dialogue guidance, atmosphere, history, maps, handouts, and other supporting content.

TEST FOR RUNNABILITY

After writing a scenario, imagine that a GM has thirty seconds during play to answer each of these questions:

- What is this NPC doing?
- What do they want?
- What do they know?
- What happens if the players threaten them?
- What happens if the PCs leave?
- What clues exist here?
- What does each clue lead to?
- What happens next if the PCs stall?
- Who is currently acting against the PCs?
- How has the situation changed since the previous scene?

If the answer requires searching through several pages of prose, the adventure needs a stronger interface.

DO NOT CONFUSE COMPLETENESS WITH RUNNABILITY

A module can contain every relevant fact and still be difficult to run.

The adventure writer's job is therefore not finished when all information exists.

The writer must also make relationships between information visible.

The GM should not need to create their own:

- clue map
- NPC motivation sheet
- event timeline
- escalation tracker
- faction summary
- investigation flowchart
- list of active threats
- "what happens next" notes

If one of these structures is necessary to understand or operate the adventure, strongly consider including it in the adventure itself.

The GM's preparation should be spent adapting the scenario to their players, not reconstructing the scenario's architecture.
